The birch log in the stone hearth popped, throwing a shower of orange sparks against the black iron fire-screen. Outside the cabin, a November gale was driving rain off the Atlantic, rattling the heavy cedar shingles and smacking against the small multipaned windows. 

The air in the room smelled of woodsmoke, old leather-bound books, damp wool, and the hot, metallic tang of an old tube-driven shortwave receiver humming on the side table.

You were sitting on the rag rug in front of the hearth, your knees pulled up, a heavy hardcover textbook open on the floor before you. You had been staring at the same paragraph in Chapter 14 for forty minutes. 

On the page, the authors had written: *The electromagnetic field propagates through the vacuum of free space, which is characterized by a permittivity $\epsilon_0$, a permeability $\mu_0$, and an intrinsic wave impedance $Z_0 \approx 377\ \Omega$. The vacuum contains no physical medium.*

You rubbed your temples, the dry paper scratching under your fingers. "It doesn't make any sense, Doc."

Across the fire, sitting in a worn leather wingback chair with a mug of hot cider between his hands, sat Dr. Thomas Henderson. He was seventy-one, with a silver beard that caught the amber glow of the embers, and forty years of experimental radio astronomy behind him. 

"What doesn't make sense, son?" Henderson asked, his voice low and gravelly, matching the steady roar of the wind in the chimney.

"The math," you said, pointing at the page. "They say space is a total, empty nothingness. A void. But then they give it a capacitive storage capacity, an inductive stiffness, and a 377-ohm opposition to flow. How can an empty void have an impedance? Nothing doesn't push back with three hundred and seventy-seven ohms of resistance when you pass a wave through it!"

Henderson took a slow sip of his cider. He set the mug down on the hearthstone with a quiet *clink*, reached into his coat pocket, and pulled out an old, tarnished brass pocket watch. He didn't open it. He just held it in his palm, letting the firelight glint off the worn engraving.

"You're suffering from the amputation, kid," Henderson said softly. "You're trying to read a story after the doctors cut the legs off the main character."

He leaned forward, the leather of his chair creaking.

"Let me tell you about Cleveland," Henderson said. "July, 1887. Case School of Applied Science. Down in the basement, below street level, where the vibration of horse-drawn wagons couldn't reach."

You closed the textbook slightly, listening.

"Albert Michelson and Edward Morley built a monster down in that cellar," Henderson continued, his eyes reflecting the flames. "They took a five-foot-square, five-hundred-pound slab of Ohio sandstone, mounted a network of heavy brass mirrors on it, and floated the whole rock in a circular iron tub filled with eight hundred pounds of liquid mercury. They wanted it so quiet that a man breathing in the hallway wouldn't shake the mirrors."

"They were looking for the Aether Wind," you said.

"They were looking for a mechanical iron cage," Henderson corrected, his voice sharpening. "They were 19th-century steam-engine builders, son. They thought space was a rigid, solid, mechanical lattice—like an iron bridge—and that the Earth was swimming through it like a boat through a pond. They expected to feel a wind blowing through the screen."

Henderson held up his thumb and forefinger an inch apart.

"Michelson spent weeks in that damp basement, staring through a tiny telescope at sodium-light interference fringes. He rotated that five-hundred-pound sandstone slab on its mercury bath, expecting the fringes to shift by four-tenths of a width as the Earth moved through the 'wind'. Do you know what he found?"

"Zero," you said.

"Point zero zero five," Henderson said. "A rounding error. No wind. No drag. No iron cage."

He sat back, staring into the heart of the fire.

"And that’s where the tragedy happened. That's where the amputation was performed. Science panicked. The theorists looked at Michelson’s null result, and instead of saying, *'Ah, our picture of a rigid, mechanical iron solid was wrong, but space is still a living ocean,'* they pulled out the bone saw."

Henderson made a sharp, slicing motion with the edge of his hand through the warm air above the hearth.

"They threw out the river because they couldn't find the wind! They said: *If there's no mechanical wind, there's no medium at all! Space is a void! Space is nothing!* And then, for the next hundred years, they spent every single day in their laboratories measuring the elasticity of the nothing, the density of the nothing, the zero-point energy of the nothing, and the three hundred and seventy-seven ohm friction of the nothing!"

He laughed—a dry, booming sound that competed with the rain on the roof.

"They amputated the Plenum, kid! They kept the patient's shoes and swore the feet were an illusion!"

You looked down at the textbook. "So what was the story before the amputation, Doc? What is the story they cut out?"

Henderson picked up his cider, the steam rising between his hands.

"The story before the cut is that you aren't an isolated rock floating in a cold, dead vacuum," he said quietly. "The story is that the space between you and me—between this cabin and the Andromeda galaxy—is $H_{\text{incoh}}$. It's the Plenum. "When I turn that radio dial, I'm not waiting for a particle to fly across the state of Vermont and physically punch the antenna. I'm inducing a phase-conduction wave through the Plenum! He stood up, walked to the multipaned window, and looked out into the storm. The black Atlantic was invisible, but the roar of the surf breaking against the granite cliffs two hundred feet below echoed through the glass.

"When two people sit in a room," Henderson said, his reflection framed by the dark glass and the rain, "or when two atoms align their spins, or when a star pulls on a planet... they aren't reaching across a void. You reached out, took the heavy hardcover textbook, and quietly closed it.

Outside, sixty miles out over the pitch-black sea, a lighthouse on a submerged reef flashed its white beam into the rain—a single, periodic pulse propagating through the 377-ohm dark, carrying its phase clean across the water to the cabin window without losing a single volt.
